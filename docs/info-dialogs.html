Section calculation disclosure🔔 Bird GenOne ranked feed of fresh megas, celebrity birds, crowd attention, leaderboard cascades, unusually busy hotspots and high-yield top patches.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: surgeBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.surgeBtn

One ranked feed of fresh megas, celebrity birds, crowd attention, leaderboard cascades, unusually busy hotspots and high-yield top patches.

Data it reads

  • the cached ABA Code 3+ snapshot last populated by Mega rarities
  • county recent + notable feeds
  • the region's Top 100 board
  • hotspot activity and the complete destination projection in the same feeds

How it is calculated

  • One SpeciesCards.small feed runs over data other sections already pay for. Sorting only reorders those in-memory rows and never fetches.
  • Buzz is the default: MEGA, CELEBRITY, CROWD, CASCADE, HOTSPOT, TOP PATCH, with newest report first inside each category. Newest ignores category severity and sorts every row by report time.
  • Every alert is one always-visible three-line card. The name cell contains two lines: the name alone, then a flush-left four-letter code, xN, linked place and linked compact date. The category badge begins a separate full-width explanation row below the headline cell, followed by the bold reason without a repeated written category prefix. The alert icon and relative age remain at the right.
  • Unseen is the default view. All reveals species already counted in the active report; switching either filter is local and never fetches.
  • Seen status is resolved once after duplicate species and their reasons are merged. Species-blind HOTSPOT alerts remain visible in both views because a place has no single bird to mark seen.
  • If the same species qualifies in several categories it renders once under the strongest category. Every qualifying reason remains in accessible category metadata; the visible bold third line explains the strongest category.
  • Each source reports whether it succeeded, failed, is still loading, is using an earlier snapshot, or has not been loaded. Successful rows and retained snapshot rows remain visible when another live feed fails, and the feed only claims nothing is happening when every applicable source succeeded with current data. A complete stale chase snapshot can still supply TOP PATCH alerts while live data refreshes; partial and failed destination views cannot.
  • The whole headline strip - image, common name or hotspot, alert icon and age - is one keyboard-actionable route to Stakeout bird or Stakeout hotspot. Bird Gen displays no rarity R marker. The four-letter code begins line two flush with the name; xN, linked place and linked newest-checklist date follow. The category badge lives only in the full-width explanation row. Bird Gen does not show the eBird species code, mileage, a Notes toggle or category destination links. Word, glyph, underline and distinct border style carry meaning without relying on colour.
  • MEGA: newness is scoped to the species within a 4-mile locality cluster, so nearby personal-location labels remain one continuing bird while a distant new site can alert. A new locality inside the covered window is news at any distance. Nearby, multiple-report and single-locality qualifiers require the bird to be within the 55-mile day-trip gate and still unseen. Far + not new or already-seen birds remain in Mega rarities and are disclosed as held back.
  • CELEBRITY: an unseen species eBird flags as notable here has 4 or more independent sightings at one hotspot or adjacent walkable hotspots, within chase distance and the last 24 hours plus the shared checklist-start grace.
  • NOTABLE, not merely unseen. Asked for as "happening now should be things local birders would talk about without having to check ebird" - and three people reporting a bird that is ordinary here is well corroborated and still nobody mentions it. An unseen-but-ordinary bird belongs in All unseen reports and Closest spots.
  • CELEBRITY has no one-report rarity exception: one mega can be worth chasing in Twitches today, but this category specifically means four independent sightings at one walkable locality.
  • Places that read as private - a named residence, a street address, bare coordinates - are never offered as somewhere to drive.
  • CROWD: distinct OBSERVERS (not checklists — one birder filing three lists is how a quiet spot fakes a crowd) in the last 36 h, against that species' trailing baseline at that place, clustered to 300 m. With no trailing baseline, the bold third line says so instead of claiming the reports are above normal or printing an infinite ratio.
  • CASCADE: 2+ of the region's top 100 adding the same species within 3 days. The board sees counties our feeds never cover and can lag a day; the actionable place and time come from a species-scoped state feed.
  • HOTSPOT: species-blind. It fires when a place gets unusually busy even if no bird was ever flagged notable. Its compact date and Newest-sort time come from the newest hot checklist that triggered the event, not from a species observation merged for detail. The place name opens Stakeout hotspot; Bird Gen performs no hidden species/checklist hydration.
  • TOP PATCH: only the highest yieldBand from a complete destination projection, meaning a score at least twice the current list median when at least four destinations can be compared. The headline opens Stakeout hotspot.
  • A surge needs two distinct observers AND two distinct checklists. Two names on one shared checklist is one observation, not corroboration.

What it cannot tell you

  • The CELEBRITY category is deliberately short: 24 hours plus checklist-start grace and your chase distance. A bird you need that has been around for a week remains in All unseen reports - the point of an alert is that it is short enough to read.
  • A report whose date cannot be read is dropped from a freshness-gated source rather than assumed fresh.
  • The baseline is measured from the data actually fetched (7 days), capped at the configured window, so a short feed cannot inflate every ratio.
  • With no trailing history at all the row reads "new here" and prints no ratio — a ratio over a zero denominator invents precision.
  • Bird Gen is a news feed, not a checklist browser. It keeps one linked newest date as evidence but renders no recent-report list, checklist drawer or hotspot species appendix; those details live in the two Stakeout sections.
Section calculation disclosure🌤 Twitch weatherWhether today's weather and tide favour going out, for the region's home coordinate.

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.wxBtn

Whether today's weather and tide favour going out, for the region's home coordinate.

Data it reads

  • NOAA forecast (US only)
  • NOAA tides for the region's station

How it is calculated

  • Sunrise, sunset and civil twilight are computed for the home coordinate, in the region's local time.
  • Tides are listed one row per WINDOW, not per turning point, because the fact that decides whether you leave the house is when the water is coming IN — reconstructing that from two adjacent turning points is work the reader should not do.
  • Rising windows are marked 👀: an incoming tide pushes waterfowl and shorebirds closer in.
  • The last window of the day runs past midnight and is kept — an overnight incoming tide is exactly when the good shorebird chases happen.

What it cannot tell you

  • Tides need a NOAA station id; inland regions leave it blank and get no tide table.
Section calculation disclosure📆 Due back soonWhich birds are about to return to the region, from seven years of archived records.

Surface: App-only section · App anchor: dueBackBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.dueBackBtn

Which birds are about to return to the region, from seven years of archived records.

Data it reads

  • The bundled arrival table (arrivals.js), harvested from GBIF once per state
  • eBird product/spplist for the region (the same cached species index the lookup uses - no extra eBird traffic)
  • GBIF occurrence/search month and day facets, for anything the table does not cover

How it is calculated

  • Takes every species ever recorded in the region (eBird's region checklist) and asks GBIF when each one historically shows up.
  • Most of that is already answered: the arrival table is harvested once in the report repo and ships with the app, so the phone only asks about species the table does not cover.
  • A species present all year has no arrival to predict and is skipped; only genuine migrants get a date.
  • "Present all year" counts months carrying at least 1% of the species' records, not months with a single straggler in them - the difference moved 106 of 295 Washington migrants out of "resident".
  • The date is the first day of the season that reaches 5% of the peak day's records, pooled across every year GBIF holds - so it is the long-term pattern, not one unusual spring.
  • Shows anything due within 30 days, plus anything that arrived in the last 14, because a bird already back is the one you can act on today.
  • 🔍 marks a bird you still need; the rest are ones you have already recorded this year.

What it cannot tell you

  • App-only: the report is built once from a fixed snapshot and committed, and 'what arrives in the next three weeks' is a question whose answer moves every day.
  • Only Washington is harvested so far. Any other region falls back to reading GBIF live, which takes a few minutes the first time; it can be stopped and resumes where it left off.
  • GBIF lags about a year, so this is the long-term pattern and never this season's actual birds - it says when to start looking, not that anything is here.
  • A species too sparse in the archive to give a stable date is left out rather than guessed at.
  • Arrival is measured for the whole state, not your chase radius: a bird can be back on the coast a week before it reaches you.
  • A winter bird whose peak is January is measured from December, so its date can read as the start of that window rather than its true arrival in autumn.

Your standing on the region's eBird Top 100, plus the board itself.

Data it reads

  • ebird.org/top100 for the region (public HTML, no API key)

How it is calculated

  • The board is fetched for the report's primary region only — one report, one region, one board.
  • eBird's default top100 ordering is already by species, so no explicit sort is requested.
  • The page self-identifies its region in a canonical link; a board that does not match is rejected and refetched rather than rendered under the wrong heading.
  • "of N eBirders" comes from a separate endpoint that needs eBird's own web token, scraped from the site bundle.

What it cannot tell you

  • eBird runs a proof-of-work bot filter on some region pages. When the token cannot be resolved the field size is omitted rather than failing the section — so a missing "of N" means degraded, not zero.
  • Each published day is a dated snapshot; eBird cannot re-serve yesterday's board.
Section calculation disclosure🧭 Pro patchesWhere the hundred busiest local birders actually go, ranked by how disproportionately they choose a place rather than by how many checklists it holds.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: patchBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.patchBtn

Where the hundred busiest local birders actually go, ranked by how disproportionately they choose a place rather than by how many checklists it holds.

Data it reads

  • data/wa/choice-patches.json, built from 730 harvested county-days (product/lists/{region}/{y}/{m}/{d})
  • eBird's ref/hotspot index, to tell a hotspot from a personal pin
  • ebird.org/top100 for the statewide board rank annotation only

How it is calculated

  • A place's score is a LIFT: the share of that birder's checklists filed there, over the share of everyone's checklists filed there. 5x means they choose it five times as often as the counties as a whole do.
  • Both shares are evidence-weighted — a 95% Wilson lower bound on the birder's share over a Wilson upper bound on the population's — so a place visited once cannot tie a place visited two hundred times.
  • Personal pins are excluded and only eBird hotspots are ranked: pins are 92% of places but under half the effort, and a birder is usually their sole visitor, which is what made them dominate a raw ranking.
  • The population is the top 100 by LOCAL EFFORT in these counties, not the statewide species board — only about a fifth of them overlap.

What it cannot tell you

  • Coverage is part of the answer: this sees only the checklists eBird publishes, so a birder who keeps their lists private looks like they have no patch. Those birders are NAMED rather than ranked low.
  • Two counties (King, Snohomish) over 365 days. A birder who birds elsewhere in the state will not appear.
  • The join key is a display NAME — eBird's day feed carries no user id — so two birders sharing a name would merge.

Section calculation disclosure🚗 Half-day patchesOutings beyond Today’s patches that still fit a half day.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: excBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.excBtn

Outings beyond Today’s patches that still fit a half day.

Data it reads

  • the same clustered unseen reports as Today’s patches
  • eBird’s county list and county bounding boxes for the active state
  • the shared travel-zone day bands

How it is calculated

  • The first open loads county names and bounds from eBird and caches each valid result permanently. A failed or malformed county is not cached and is retried on refresh.
  • County acquisition is derived from Home: include counties whose nearest bounding-box edge is inside the half-day band’s straight-line ceiling. Moving Home recomputes the selection from the same cached metadata.
  • Bounding boxes decide only which feeds are worth buying. Each actual hotspot is classified from its own coordinates using straight mileage plus route friction; quick and half-day bands stay here.
  • The board starts beyond the exact adaptive boundary Today’s patches used, so one hotspot cannot appear in both.
  • Rarities count ×3, needs-verification keeps its reduced weight, and distance decay makes a farther site earn the drive.

What it cannot tell you

  • A county box can include inaccessible terrain near Home, so scope deliberately overfetches. It never assigns a travel band to the whole county.
  • The county scope is selected from Home. Here and Find may rerank loaded cards, but they do not silently acquire a different state-sized data set.
  • The first cold catalog is expensive, but it is paid only when Half-day or Full-day is opened and never again for valid county bounds.

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.fullDayBtn

Farther hotspots worth planning a full birding day around.

Data it reads

  • the same API-derived county catalog as Half-day patches
  • recent and notable observations from counties inside the full-day acquisition envelope
  • the shared travel-zone day bands

How it is calculated

  • The county envelope comes from the full-day band’s straight-line ceiling, not a Washington county list or a hand-picked destination list.
  • Each hotspot must classify as full day from its own coordinates. Half-day options remain in Half-day; anything in the trip band is not offered as a day outing.
  • This board ranks independently, so dense nearby parks cannot bury the long-drive options the section exists to compare.
  • Every card states full day in words and keeps route friction such as a ferry or seasonal pass as an additional non-colour signal.

What it cannot tell you

  • County edge distance is a fetch envelope, not a driving-time promise.
  • A first cold load may take several minutes because every selected county contributes recent and notable feeds. Cached bounds and in-session eBird responses make later opens cheaper.
  • The model still records known route-calibration gaps such as Port Townsend; it does not invent a routing API.
Section calculation disclosure🚶 Find local patchesAn impulse detour: the richest hotspots a few minutes from where you are.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: quickBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.quickBtn

An impulse detour: the richest hotspots a few minutes from where you are.

Data it reads

  • ref/hotspot/geo around the chosen anchor

How it is calculated

  • Pick the anchor — home, your current location, or a place you search for. Home is the default, which is what the Markdown report uses.
  • Hotspots are scored on all-time species with a distance decay, so a 40-species puddle two minutes away does not beat a real site.
  • But the LIST IS SORTED BY DISTANCE, not by score: score decides which spots make the cut, proximity decides the order.
  • The radius starts at 5 mi and widens to 10 then 15 rather than print an empty section in a sparse region.
  • Hotspots birded in the last 10 days are penalised but not removed.

What it cannot tell you

  • This is about places, not birds — it does not know what is being seen there today.
  • In the app the origin is yours to choose, so the rows can differ from the Markdown report, which can only ever rank from a fixed anchor.
Section calculation disclosure📍 Closest patchesThe nearest place holding a bird you still need.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: targetsBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.targetsBtn

The nearest place holding a bird you still need.

Data it reads

  • /data/obs/{county}/recent for each county in the report
  • the notable feeds
  • the geo feeds around home

How it is calculated

  • Start from every observation in the report's own county and geo feeds — this is county-scoped, not statewide.
  • Drop anything you have already seen this year, using the taxonomy parent chain so a subspecies of a bird you have seen does not read as unseen.
  • Apply the chaseability gate (see limits) — a bird you cannot legally go and look at is not a target.
  • Cluster what remains to 250 m so ten reports from one pond become one place.
  • Measure each cluster from home. There used to be a second fixed anchor, the workplace, so a spot by the office ranked on its short hop; it was retired because a stored workplace is a guess about where you will be, and the app's current-location anchor is not.
  • Keep one record per species per cluster (the most recent), sort rarities first, then sort the clusters closest-first.
  • Show everything within 30 mi, at least the 10 nearest, never more than 40.

What it cannot tell you

  • The chaseability gate rejects a location whose NAME says it is closed — a parenthesised 'private', 'restricted', 'no public access' and similar. That is checked before anything else, so even a well-attested stakeout at such a place is dropped.
  • A location eBird marks private is still shown when it qualifies as a stakeout: 3+ distinct checklists within 300 m, which is what a roadside viewing point outside someone's yard looks like in the data. That is why a residential address can legitimately appear.
  • THE BIG ONE: the per-county 'recent' feed returns exactly ONE observation per species. So a needed bird has at most a handful of known locations in the whole dataset, and the nearest of those may be far from the nearest place it actually is. This section is sparse by construction, not by accident.
Section calculation disclosure🔎 Stakeout birdAsk where ANY bird is being seen — including ones already on your year list.

Surface: App-only section · App anchor: spLookupBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.spLookupBtn

Ask where ANY bird is being seen — including ones already on your year list.

Data it reads

  • eBird product/spplist for the active region and, only after an empty match, each parent region in order
  • data/obs/{region}/recent/{code}, the per-species observation feed
  • ref/taxonomy/ebird, fetched directly with your key and cached as a compact on-device model for the identification ladder

How it is calculated

  • Typed text is matched against common and scientific names. The active region is searched first; only if it has no match does the search climb through its parent regions, stopping at the first useful level.
  • A parent-region result stays under an Outside {active region} heading and each row says it is not on the active regional list. Opening it still asks the original report for sightings, so a taxonomy match can never masquerade as a local observation.
  • If every regional level misses, Search full eBird taxonomy is offered as an explicit action rather than silently changing the search to the whole world.
  • One active-region match goes straight to the bird rather than showing a menu of one; fallback matches keep their visible scope boundary even when there is only one.
  • Observations are grouped to one shared HotspotCards.small row per location, keeping that place's most recent checklist, count and evidence.
  • Sorted by date (most recent first) or by distance from home — the two different questions 'is it still around' and 'how far must I drive'.
  • The first 25 places paint immediately. Show more appends the next batch into the same list without refetching, nesting a second list or accepting a stale control from an older species lookup.
  • Looks back 30 days, wider than the chase feeds, because a lookup is asked about birds that may not be present today.
  • A visible Identification path starts loading independently of the sightings request. It aligns class, order, family, genus and species from broad to specific, groups equivalent spuh labels side by side at one coverage level, marks coverage-limited wording, and links every spuh into Spuh finder.
  • Compare possible species finds the minimal published spuh set containing every selection. Because spuh sets overlap, it can return more than one equally narrow answer rather than forcing a false single-parent tree.

What it cannot tell you

  • App-only: a Markdown report cannot take a query, and printing every species' places for a region would be a phone book, not a section.
  • Absence is an answer, not an error — no rows means it is not being reported right now, which is exactly what you asked.
  • The name lookup can identify a bird outside the region, but the sightings answer remains scoped to the active report. A fallback match may therefore lead to the honest answer that there are no local reports in the active window.
  • The per-species feed returns only the most recent report at each place. One row per place is the endpoint's limit, not the bird's full history; repeated visits at the same place may be omitted.
  • The spuh answer proves taxonomy containment, not that the field identification is correct. It is safe only if every plausible species was selected.
  • Seventy-nine labels are shown with a dashed Coverage limit warning and are never selected automatically: 73 parenthetical glosses whose narrower membership is not published (58 share coverage with another spuh and 15 are lone labels), five named subfamilies, and the mixed Buteo/eagle group.
Section calculation disclosure🌳 Spuh finderBrowse uncertain bird IDs, see what each one contains, and compare possible species without forcing overlapping groups into a false tree.

Surface: App-only section · App anchor: spuhBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.spuhBtn

Browse uncertain bird IDs, see what each one contains, and compare possible species without forcing overlapping groups into a false tree.

Data it reads

  • eBird ref/taxonomy/ebird, called directly from the device with your API key
  • the app's existing attributed Wikipedia/Wikimedia photo pipeline, for a clearly labelled example candidate image

How it is calculated

  • The full taxonomy is compacted on the device to concrete species, spuh labels and reportAs aliases, then cached in IndexedDB for 30 days. No eBird taxonomy bundle ships in the public repository.
  • Each spuh is resolved to its exact published candidate-species set. Genus, family and order lists are unions; twelve scientific species-list rows are parsed species by species rather than widened to their whole family.
  • Labels with identical candidate sets share one coverage class. Minimal strict subset and superset relations form a DAG, because real groups overlap.
  • The hierarchy, candidate set and narrower groups lead the result. The information button holds the generated definition, source boundary and example-image source so repeat users do not have to scroll past explanatory prose.
  • The example image is one possible candidate and says so in words; it is never called the spuh or the most common member.
  • Compare possible species returns zero, one or several minimal published sets containing every selected bird.

What it cannot tell you

  • App-only: the archived Markdown report cannot take a search or a set of candidate species.
  • The taxonomy tells you membership, not field evidence. A narrow shared group is not proof that the selected possibilities are complete.
  • Coverage-limited labels remain searchable and visible but are never automatic recommendations.
  • The first use needs an eBird API key and one full taxonomy call. Later opens use the 30-day on-device cache.
Section calculation disclosure🏞 Stake out a hotspotEverything about ONE place you have already chosen, rather than which place to choose.

Surface: App-only section · App anchor: stakeHsBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.stakeHsBtn

Everything about ONE place you have already chosen, rather than which place to choose.

Data it reads

  • eBird ref/hotspot/{region} for the name search, cached
  • eBird product/lists/{locId}, one call, which carries every recent checklist header AND its observer

How it is calculated

  • The PATTERN leads, not today rows. How many checklists, across how many days, and what a visit is typically worth are true all day - unlike what was seen today, which is empty every morning by eBird own design.
  • MEASURED 2026-08-22 at 10:10: three target species returned ZERO reports from that day across the whole state. eBird embargoes checklists for an hour (its hotspot page says so) and people submit hours after the walk.
  • WHO BIRDS HERE is counted from the same single call, because product/lists carries userDisplayName. The patch-regular list is a different and more useful thing than the county top 100: the person to ask about a place is whoever actually birds it. Cedar River mouth reads Eric Hope 30.
  • Checklists are shown newest-first, which is the order product/lists returns - measured at three depths, 5, 50 and 200.

What it cannot tell you

  • It does not enumerate hidden checklists. Both species_recent and /data/obs/{loc}/recent/{code} collapse to ONE row per location - measured across 14 species at two hotspots, including owls, which was the sharpest test because owlers often use custom pins. Only /data/obs/{loc}/historic/{y}/{m}/{d} returns them all, at one call per DAY, and that cost is not spent here.
  • Checklist comments are not shown because they are in no eBird endpoint at all - hasComments measured true on ZERO of 2,860 rows.
Section calculation disclosure⭐ Iconic spots near meWhich places near you are unusually good for one particular bird — the signal eBird's own hotspot pages call Iconic Birds.

Surface: App-only section · App anchor: iconicBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

Prose source: www/section-docs.json · docs.iconicBtn

Which places near you are unusually good for one particular bird — the signal eBird's own hotspot pages call Iconic Birds.

Data it reads

  • eBird ref/hotspot/{region}, the cached hotspot directory, for places within your chase radius
  • GBIF occurrence facets over the eBird Observation Dataset — keyless, so this costs no eBird quota
  • GBIF geocode/reverse, to resolve each hotspot to the county it sits in

How it is calculated

  • For each hotspot, ONE faceted GBIF call returns every species' share of the records in a 2 km box around it, together with the box total. A second call does the same for the whole county, and is shared by every hotspot inside it.
  • The multiplier is the bird's share of records at the place divided by its share across the county. Against eBird's own Iconic Birds panel at Mann Rd, Sultan this reproduces eBird's exact ordering: Yellow-breasted Chat, then Western Kingbird, then Lazuli Bunting, then Eastern Kingbird.
  • Measured across the WHOLE YEAR, not the current season. eBird's August-only view at Bolt Creek Burn omits the Northern House Wren the site is known for, because it is a May–June bird there; every row therefore states which months its records fall in.
  • Two views of one scan: by BIRD keeps a row per bird-and-place pairing, so the same bird can appear for several places; by PLACE ranks hotspots by their single best bird.

What it cannot tell you

  • App-only. The numbers are measured against YOUR home and YOUR chase radius, which a Markdown report generated hours earlier cannot ask for.
  • Two evidence floors, and both matter. A box with under 200 bird records has no norm to compare against, and a species with fewer than 10 records there cannot support a ratio — without the second, Bolt Creek Burn reports a House Wren at 82× off THREE records, which is the same artifact eBird itself prints as ‘130×, observed 1/1 years’.
  • Historical odds, not sightings. A high multiplier says the bird is reliably reported there, not that it is there today — there is no checklist behind a row.
  • Bounded to the 12 nearest hotspots and the top 4 birds at each, so a scan stays fast; GBIF's eBird snapshot also lags roughly a year, so very recent sites read thin.
Section calculation disclosure🔥 Hot patchesHotspots getting unusually busy in the last 30 days.

Surface: App section and archived-report section · App anchor: hotBtn

Context: section results and any live warning vary by report and fetched data. This entry shows authored prose only.

The megararities that are DUE here — rare enough to be a record, and not found yet this year. Scope it to your county for targets you might actually break.

Data it reads

  • a bundled list computed offline from GBIF, refreshed on app release
  • your region, and which scope you picked — the whole state, or one county
  • today's month, to rank by what is plausible NOW

How it is calculated

  • Counts DISTINCT YEARS a species has been recorded here, never observations — one twitched bird inflates a row count 12-17x, so Terek Sandpiper's 15 Washington 'records' are one bird over two days.
  • Uses a 10-year window, because eBird's growth means an all-time count compares eras that are not comparable. Measured: this roughly triples the hit rate and halves the list. Washington's records committee reached the same rule independently — its review list is 'birds with 20 or fewer records in the previous 10-year period'.
  • Adds evidence from ecologically similar regions, which is the only way a bird with NO local history can be predicted at all. The Terek found at Stanwood in July 2026 had one prior precedent: Sooke, BC, the same fortnight in 1987.
  • At COUNTY scope the comparison region is the parent state, because a vagrant loose in Washington is loose in Washington. Measured: of the 132 species recorded in Washington but never in King County, the state reaches all 132 in one query while the three most similar counties reach 40.
  • Ranks by the odds for THIS MONTH, not by rarity. A 4% annual chance concentrated into one fortnight is a chase decision; spread across a year it is trivia.
  • Excludes escaped cagebirds by family, plus an explicit list of collection birds that families cannot express — Black Swan is a waterfowl, Golden Pheasant a phasianid, so excluding those families would delete Emperor Goose and Sooty Grouse with them.
  • The exclusions carry a per-region exemption, because countability is regional: Common Waxbill and Rose-ringed Parakeet are established and countable in Hawaii while being escapees on the mainland.

What it cannot tell you

  • It is a WATCHLIST, not a forecast. At state scope roughly one in six listed birds turns up within two years. A county record is a lower bar, which is why the county list is longer — no separate hit rate has been measured for it.
  • It cannot see a bird nobody has ever reported near you. A first record with no precedent anywhere in the region is exactly the case no history can predict.
  • Hawaii has no ecological peer in the ABA area, so its list can only contain birds already recorded there — a Hawaiian vagrant arrives off the Pacific, and no mainland state predicts it.
  • The list is bundled and only changes when the app updates, because the model needs decades of data the phone does not have. Only counties the reports cover are bundled.
  • A species rare next door because it is endangered and local, rather than because it wanders, can still slip in.
  • The escapee list is hand-authored, because no machine-readable source survived measurement — so a collection bird nobody has listed yet can still appear. African Collared-Dove does, for King County.

One feed ranks every active alert. Buzz orders the six categories — mega, celebrity, crowd, cascade, hotspot, top patch — then uses recency inside each category. Newest ignores category severity and orders every row by its latest report time.

Hotspot alerts stay visible in Unseen mode because they describe a place, not one bird.

If a source fails or the mega snapshot has not been loaded, the feed names that gap instead of claiming nothing is happening. A complete retained snapshot may still supply TOP PATCH rows while live data refreshes; partial or failed destination views may not.

🦅 ABA Code 3+ megas

Continent-level rarities — ABA Code 3, 4 and 5 — currently reported in this region, one line per species, newest report kept.

This category never fetches. It shows whatever the Rare birds section last loaded, and prints how old that is. The ABA alert is a scraped continent-wide page, and fetching it every time you opened Happening now is what put this section on a 30-second rate-limit timeout. So the section you open deliberately pays that cost, and this lane never does. If it looks empty, open Rare birds once.

New locality, or near and unseen. A first record inside a four-mile locality cluster is news at any distance. Nearby hotspot labels inside that radius remain one continuing bird. The other qualifiers — nearby, multiple reports, or multiple reports at one hotspot — require the bird to be within the 55-mile day-trip gate and still missing from your year list. A far bird that is not new, or one you have already seen, stays in Mega rarities instead of alerting again here.

For photos, state history and the rarity evidence, open Rare birds: this feed keeps one compact row per bird.

🎯 Celebrity Birds

Birds eBird flags as notable in this region that are not on your year list, with 4 or more independent sightings at one hotspot or adjacent walkable hotspots, within chase distance and the last 24 hours plus checklist-start grace.

Notable, not merely unseen. A bird earns this category by being unusual here and drawing a real local stakeout. An unseen-but-ordinary bird belongs in All unseen reports and Closest spots. A one-report mega belongs in Twitches today; CELEBRITY has no reviewed-rarity exemption.

Within this category the unified feed uses the newest report time, just as its Buzz and Newest descriptions state.

Every report-specific fact on a row comes from the same newest report — place, distance, count, date and checklist link. Private addresses are never listed.

🐦 Species drawing a crowd

Distinct observers — not checklists, because one birder filing three lists is how a quiet spot fakes a crowd — at one spot in the last 36 hours, measured against what that species normally draws at that place.

It fires at 4+ observers and 4× the local norm, or — for a bird with no trailing history at all — at 2 independent observers on two different checklists.

With no trailing history there is no ratio to quote. A ratio over a zero baseline would invent precision, so the bold third line names the missing baseline instead.

Rarities are deliberately excluded: they already have their own sections, and this category exists to catch the birds those miss.

🏆 Species cascading through the leaderboard

eBird’s Top 100 board prints, for each birder, the most recent species they added this year. Group that column by bird and it becomes a chase board: when several of the top 100 add the same species within a few days, that bird is demonstrably findable right now.

That is the whole rule — 2+ of the top 100 within 3 days. It is not a rarity feed, so a bird can appear here without being flagged rare anywhere, and a mega nobody has ticked yet will not appear at all.

This category sees the whole region, including counties your own feeds never cover — which is its real value — but the board is cached daily, so it can lag about a day.

The board names the bird and the date and nothing else, so the place and time on each row come from a species-scoped state feed. That is where to actually go.

📍 Hotspots unusually busy

These are places, not birds. The category is species-blind, so it fires even when whatever everyone is looking at was never flagged notable — a locally common bird draws no rarity alert however big the crowd gets. That is the one signal the other lanes cannot produce.

Each spot is measured against its own trailing norm, so an always-busy park is not news; a quiet one suddenly filling up is. A place with no trailing history is unmeasured, not busy, so it is dropped rather than called new.
